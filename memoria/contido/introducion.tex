\section{Motivation}
Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) have been at the forefront of computer science research for the past few decades, and the idea of building artificial systems inspired by the way the brain processes information remains a prominent research focus. In recent years, deep learning and transformer architectures have advanced substantially, driven by their remarkable results and by the commercialization of General Purpose Artificial Intelligence (GPAI). Nonetheless, these models suffer from two major shortcomings: their high energy and environmental cost, and the large amounts of data or environment interactions they require for training. This energy cost has given rise to initiatives such as Green AI \cite{schwartz2020green}, which advocates for computational efficiency as an evaluation criterion alongside accuracy. Neuroevolutionary algorithms offer a gradient-free alternative: they optimise neural networks with evolutionary search instead of backpropagation. In particular, the NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies (NEAT) algorithm \cite{stanley2002} improves on earlier methods by evolving network structure together with weights, and it was shown to solve benchmark control problems such as double pole balancing more efficiently than earlier neuroevolution methods, as detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}.

The practical application of this project is inspired by a well-known demonstration of NEAT playing Super Mario World, an implementation its creator named MarI/O \cite{sethbling2015mario}. It is an informal demonstration rather than a controlled study, but it illustrates that NEAT needs neither a labelled training dataset nor a model of the environment: MarI/O starts without knowing the controls, discovers movement by trial and error, and evolves until it can clear the level. This addresses the first shortcoming, the dependence on large training datasets. This project uses Super Mario Bros instead of Super Mario World because it is available as a Python environment (Section~\ref{sec:objectives-main}) and is less hardware dependent, so that the measured behaviour is closer to what a resource-limited robot controller would show.

However, standard NEAT does not address the energy problem on its own. To address it, this project combines NEAT with two biologically inspired principles: Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) \cite{gerstner2014} and Reward-Modulated Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (R-STDP) \cite{mozafari2018,izhikevich2007}. SNNs are built on the premise that biological neurons communicate through discrete electrical pulses called spikes. As an analogy, a standard ANN behaves like an open tap, through which water (signal) flows continuously; an SNN behaves like a tap that is almost closed, in which water accumulates and falls only as a single drop once a certain level is reached. R-STDP is intended to complement the evolutionary process: while NEAT evolves the topology and the initial weights across generations, R-STDP adapts the synaptic weights during gameplay. This combination is expected to give the agent more adaptability within an episode; whether it also helps the agent to transfer to new levels or similar tasks without restarting training from scratch is a question that this project does not claim to answer in advance.

The practical relevance of this architecture extends to constrained engineering environments. A rover or search-and-rescue drone could use local plasticity to adapt after a component failure without relying on a remote server. Remote Internet-of-Things (IoT) sensors could remain mostly idle and compute only when an acoustic or environmental anomaly is detected. Medical devices such as neural implants or smart pacemakers could process changing biological signals locally, while wearable devices could personalise their models without continuously transmitting private data to the cloud. This project evaluates the approach only in a simulated game, but these scenarios illustrate the potential uses of such a technology.

As modern technology increasingly shifts toward edge computing, autonomous robotics and IoT devices, the demand for AI systems that adapt in real time with low power consumption is growing. Evaluating spiking networks with R-STDP in a dynamic software environment such as Super Mario Bros therefore addresses a gap in the literature (Chapter~\ref{ch:sota}) and contributes a first step towards controllers that could later be deployed on neuromorphic hardware.

\section{Objectives}

\subsection{Main objective}
\label{sec:objectives-main}
The primary objective of this project is to develop an autonomous program, commonly referred to as an ``agent'', capable of navigating and completing levels of Super Mario Bros. In this context, an agent is a computational entity that repeatedly observes its surroundings and makes decisions to achieve a goal. The agent learns from a reward signal, that is, through trial, error and feedback from the environment, as in Reinforcement Learning (RL). In this project the reward guides two mechanisms: the evolutionary search (through the fitness of each genome) and the local synaptic adaptation within an episode (through R-STDP).

To address the energy cost of conventional networks, the project replaces the continuous-valued neurons used by standard NEAT with a biologically inspired Spiking Neural Network (SNN) built from Leaky Integrate-and-Fire neurons. The agent's within-episode learning is governed by Reward-Modulated Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (R-STDP), which lets it adapt its synaptic weights while it plays.

The goal is to evaluate whether an agent built on these principles can adapt during play while reducing the estimated energy consumption relative to a standard NEAT baseline.

The experimental environment is the Super Mario Bros implementation provided by the gym-super-mario-bros package in Python \cite{gym_super_mario_bros}.

For this document, \emph{comparable performance} is defined operationally: the spiking agent should reach at least 90\% of the baseline mean distance and completion rate, while requiring no more than 10\% additional frames to complete the level. These metrics will be reported as mean and standard deviation over five randomised seeds on Level 1-1. Training energy is outside the primary energy metric; the total number of evaluated frames will nevertheless be reported for transparency.

\subsection{Specific objectives}
To achieve the main objective, the project is divided into the following sub-goals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Establish a baseline:} implement and train a standard NEAT algorithm to play Super Mario Bros, to serve as a performance and architectural baseline.
    \item \textbf{Implement spiking mechanics:} replace the continuous activation functions of standard NEAT (e.g., sigmoid) with a discrete-time Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) \cite{gerstner2014} neuron model, and define a spike-encoding method for the environment observation.
    \item \textbf{Integrate local plasticity:} incorporate R-STDP so that the agent's synaptic weights adapt during an episode according to the environmental reward.
    \item \textbf{Evaluate efficiency:} compare the performance and the estimated inference energy of the Spiking-NEAT agent with those of the standard NEAT baseline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Chosen approach}
The approach is organised around three components, described here through the metaphor of the architect, the engine and the teacher.

\textbf{The architect (topology).} The structure of the agent's network is generated with NEAT. NEAT improves upon earlier Topology and Weight Evolving Artificial Neural Networks (TWEANNs) through historical markings (innovation numbers), speciation and incremental growth from minimal structures. Because NEAT has been validated on control benchmarks such as double pole balancing \cite{stanley2002}, and because it was the algorithm used in the MarI/O demonstration, it is a natural choice to grow the network topology in this project.

\textbf{The engine (neuron model).} Standard NEAT typically uses a continuous sigmoidal activation function bounded in $[0,1]$. Because activations are continuous floating-point values, the hardware must perform a Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) operation for every synapse at every time step, even when a neuron's activation is close to zero. This unconditional computation is what drives the energy cost discussed in the previous section. To reduce it, the network is turned into an SNN by using the Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) model \cite{gerstner2014}. The LIF model is a mathematical abstraction of a biological neuron with three mechanics: \emph{integrate} (accumulate incoming signals), \emph{leak} (the membrane potential decays over time) and \emph{fire} (emit a binary spike when the potential reaches a threshold, then reset). Extending the tap analogy, the neuron is a bucket with a small hole: the tap is the synaptic current (integrate), the water level is the membrane potential, the hole is the leak, and the bucket overflowing and being emptied is the spike (fire). Since the signals between neurons are binary, the contribution of a spike to the next neuron is a single addition instead of a multiplication, and nothing is transmitted when a neuron does not fire. If activity is sparse, this reduces the number of expensive operations. The membrane potential of every neuron must still be updated at each time step, and this cost is included explicitly in the energy model of Section~\ref{sec:energy-estimation}.

\textbf{The teacher (learning rule).} In standard NEAT, synaptic weights change only through random mutation between generations, which can be an inefficient way to refine them. The project therefore adds R-STDP as the ``teacher''. R-STDP is a biologically inspired, reward-driven rule: it keeps a temporary trace of which synapses were recently active just before a postsynaptic spike, and when a reward signal arrives, it strengthens or weakens those synapses depending on whether the reward was better or worse than expected. It is inspired by the role of dopamine in the brain, and it favours connections that preceded rewarded behaviour.

NEAT evolves the topology and the initial weights, while R-STDP adapts the effective weights during each episode. The adapted weights are reset to the genome-encoded initial values at the beginning of each new episode and are not written back to the genome. Several episodes per generation will therefore be evaluated so that the effect of within-episode adaptation can influence fitness. As an ablation, a Lamarckian variant will also be evaluated, in which the weights adapted by R-STDP at the end of an episode are written back to the genome so that offspring inherit them (as opposed to the main design, where the adaptation is discarded after each episode).
