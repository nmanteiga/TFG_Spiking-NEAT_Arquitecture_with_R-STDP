\chapter{Theoretical Background}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter introduces the theoretical and mathematical foundations needed to follow the rest of this document. It describes the models and algorithms behind neuroevolution, Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) and biologically inspired learning rules, and it explains why the three are combined in the proposed Spiking-NEAT architecture for dynamic control tasks. Related work is reviewed separately in Chapter~\ref{ch:sota}.

\section{Neuroevolution and Genetic Algorithms}

The standard method for training Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) is the backpropagation algorithm. Its purpose is to optimise a network's predictions by systematically adjusting its weights and biases to minimise a measure of error. In its classical, supervised form it learns from examples for which the correct output is known.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{imaxes/backpropagation.png}
    \caption{Architecture of a feedforward neural network illustrating the forward pass of activations and the backward propagation of error adjustments for weight optimization. Adapted from GeeksforGeeks.}
    \label{fig:backpropagation}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:backpropagation}, training operates in four phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Forward pass:} the input propagates forward through the network's layers. Each node applies a weighted sum and a nonlinear function until the final layer outputs a prediction.
    \item \textbf{Error calculation:} a loss function measures the discrepancy (the error) between the prediction and the ``ground truth'' provided by the training dataset.
    \item \textbf{Backward pass:} the algorithm computes the gradient of the loss and moves backward through the network, assigning to each weight and bias its share of the error.
    \item \textbf{Optimizer step:} an optimizer uses these gradients to update the weights so that the next prediction improves.
\end{enumerate}

Gradient descent is very powerful for supervised tasks such as image classification, but it has two requirements: it needs a large set of labelled examples to compute the loss, and it requires differentiable activation functions to compute the gradients.

In dynamic control problems, such as autonomous robotics or video games like Super Mario Bros, there is no dataset of ``perfect moves''. The agent only receives a sparse reward that tells it, after the fact, how well it did. Backpropagation can still be applied in this setting, as deep reinforcement learning shows \cite{mnih2015human}, but it then needs a very large number of environment interactions and still requires differentiable networks. Neuroevolution (NE) is an alternative that avoids gradients altogether.

Neuroevolution applies the principles of natural selection to neural networks. Instead of training a single network, it uses a Genetic Algorithm (GA) to maintain a ``population'' of candidate networks. Each network is evaluated in the environment and receives a score called its \emph{fitness}. The weakest networks are discarded, while the best ones are selected to produce the next generation through \emph{crossover} (combining the genes of two parents) and \emph{mutation} (random changes to weights or structure). Since fitness is computed only from the outcome of an episode, neither gradients nor labelled data are needed.

\subsection{TWEANNs}

Early neuroevolution systems optimised only the synaptic weights of a network whose topology was fixed by a human designer. Choosing a good architecture for a complex task is difficult, which motivated Topology and Weight Evolving Artificial Neural Networks (TWEANNs): algorithms that evolve both the connection weights and the structure of the network. Stanley and Miikkulainen \cite{stanley2002} identify three problems that limited earlier TWEANNs, and that NEAT was designed to solve.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Competing conventions (permutation) problem.} Two parent networks may solve the same problem while encoding their solutions with their hidden nodes in a different order. When such parents are crossed over (Figure~\ref{fig:competing_conventions}), the offspring often lose or duplicate parts of the solution, because the algorithm has no way to align the mismatched hidden nodes. Crossing two good parents can therefore produce a damaged offspring.
    \item \textbf{Unprotected innovation.} A newly added structure, such as a hidden node, usually lowers fitness at first, until its weights are tuned. In a single unified population, such innovations are quickly eliminated by older and simpler networks whose weights are already tuned. A single pool also lets the initially fittest individuals take over the population, which reduces diversity.
    \item \textbf{Topological bloat.} To ensure diversity, early methods started from populations of random, complex topologies. This filled the genomes with unjustified connections from the first generation and enlarged the search space unnecessarily.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{imaxes/tweanns1.png}
    \caption[The competing conventions problem.]{The competing conventions problem. Two networks that compute the same function but list their hidden units in a different order are incompatible for crossover: both single-point recombinations lose one of the three components of the solution. Reproduced from Figure~1 of~\cite{stanley2002}.}
    \label{fig:competing_conventions}
\end{figure}

As Figure~\ref{fig:competing_conventions} shows, crossing over two functionally equivalent networks with different internal orderings produces offspring that miss information (for example, node C or node A).

These three problems made it hard for TWEANNs to scale to larger tasks. NEAT addresses each of them with one mechanism.

\subsection{NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies (NEAT)}

NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies (NEAT) \cite{stanley2002} addresses the limitations above through three mechanisms: crossover based on historical markings, protection of structural innovation through speciation, and incremental growth from minimal structures. Together, they allow evolution to optimise and complexify solutions at the same time.

\subsubsection{Innovation numbers}
The mechanism that resolves the competing conventions problem is the use of historical markings, or innovation numbers. In biology, homology allows organisms with different genetic structures to recombine because their genes share a common origin. NEAT uses an analogous idea: every new structural mutation (a new connection or a new hidden node) that appears in the population is assigned a unique, increasing integer, its global innovation number. Two genes with the same innovation number therefore descend from the same original mutation.

This marking turns the problem of comparing different topologies into a linear alignment of gene lists. When two parents are crossed over, their genomes are aligned by innovation number (Figure~\ref{fig:innovation_numbers}) and the genes are classified into three types:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Matching genes:} genes with the same innovation number in both parents. The offspring inherits each of them from a randomly chosen parent.
    \item \textbf{Disjoint genes:} genes present in only one parent, whose innovation number lies within the range of the other parent's innovation numbers.
    \item \textbf{Excess genes:} genes present in only one parent, whose innovation number lies beyond the range of the other parent's innovation numbers (at the end of the genome).
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{imaxes/tweanns2.png}
    \caption[Matching genomes with innovation numbers.]{Crossover with innovation numbers. The numbers at the top of each gene show which genes of the two parents match, so their genomes can be aligned without analysing the topology. Matching genes are inherited randomly; disjoint and excess genes come from the fitter parent (here equal fitness is assumed, so they are inherited randomly too). Reproduced from Figure~4 of~\cite{stanley2002}.}
    \label{fig:innovation_numbers}
\end{figure}

Disjoint and excess genes are inherited from the fitter parent; if both parents have equal fitness, they are inherited randomly. In addition, there is a preset probability that a gene remains disabled in the offspring if it is disabled in either parent. With this alignment, networks of very different sizes and topologies can be recombined without losing track of which gene performs which function.

\FloatBarrier
\subsubsection{Speciation}
The second mechanism is speciation, also known as niching. When a structural mutation is added, performance often drops temporarily until the new weights are optimised. If the whole population competes in a single pool, these new topologies are driven to extinction by older, simpler networks whose weights are already tuned.

To prevent this, NEAT divides the population into species according to topological similarity, which it measures by using the innovation numbers described above: genomes with many matching genes are placed in the same species, whereas genomes with many disjoint or excess genes are placed in different ones.

Within each species, NEAT applies explicit fitness sharing: individuals in the same niche share their fitness, so that a large species cannot take over the whole population. Consequently, new structures compete mainly against similar genomes rather than against the whole population, which gives them the time to optimise their weights before they are judged.

\subsubsection{Minimal structures}
The third mechanism concerns how evolution starts. NEAT begins with a population of minimal networks that have no hidden nodes: the inputs are connected directly to the outputs. Structure is added gradually, through mutations that insert new connections and new hidden nodes. A new structure persists only if it improves fitness enough to survive selection.

Growing from minimal structures keeps the search space small at the beginning and tends to produce compact networks, because added complexity must justify itself. It also shows that evolution can optimise and complexify solutions at the same time, in analogy to biological evolution.

\subsubsection{Why NEAT?}
Gradient-based deep reinforcement learning can play games such as Super Mario Bros, but it needs very large numbers of environment interactions \cite{mnih2015human}. Neuroevolution needs no gradients and no labelled dataset, only a fitness score per episode. Stanley and Miikkulainen \cite{stanley2002} report, through ablation studies and benchmark comparisons on the XOR problem and on double pole balancing, that NEAT was more efficient than the earlier neuroevolution methods they compared against. They attribute this to the three mechanisms above, which together prevent the three problems of TWEANNs.

For these reasons, and because it is the algorithm used in the MarI/O demonstration \cite{sethbling2015mario}, NEAT is chosen in this project to grow the network topology and as the baseline for the subsequent integration of spiking neurons and R-STDP.

\subsubsection{Limitations}
Standard NEAT networks rely on continuous activation functions, such as the sigmoid function, whose outputs lie in $[0, 1]$. Because activations are continuous floating-point values, the hardware must perform a Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) operation for every synapse at every time step, even when a node's activation is close to zero.

This unconditional computation makes inference energy-intensive, which is a problem for edge-computing scenarios such as autonomous robotics, aerospace applications and Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices, where energy budgets are tight. This motivates replacing the continuous neurons with spiking ones.

\section{Green AI and Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs)}

\subsection{Green AI}

The rapid rise and commercialization of AI and GPAI has been accompanied by growing concern about the computational cost of training and running these systems. Schwartz et al.\ distinguish two research cultures. ``Red AI refers to AI research that seeks to obtain state-of-the-art results in accuracy (or related measures) through the use of massive computational power---essentially `buying' stronger results'' \cite{schwartz2020green}. Green AI, in contrast, advocates for treating computational cost and energy efficiency as evaluation criteria alongside accuracy \cite{schwartz2020green}. This project follows the Green AI view and reports energy as a core metric next to performance.

Schwartz et al.\ describe the cost of producing an AI result $R$ as proportional to the cost of executing the model on one example, the size of the dataset, and the number of hyperparameter experiments:

\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cost}(R) \propto E \cdot D \cdot H,
\end{equation}

where $E$ is the execution cost for one example, $D$ is the dataset size, and $H$ is the number of experiments.

Conventional networks are expensive to execute in part because they use continuous floating-point weights and activations, so the hardware performs a MAC operation for every active synapse at every time step. The activation function is the nonlinear function that maps a node's weighted input to its output; widely used examples are the sigmoid and the ReLU, both of which produce continuous values.

The biological brain does not operate like an open tap: neurons exchange discrete pulses. A biological neuron has three main parts: the dendrites, which receive input; the soma (cell body), which integrates it; and the axon, which transmits the output \cite{gerstner2014}. When the accumulated voltage at the soma is sufficiently high, the neuron emits an action potential, a brief pulse called a spike. A sequence of spikes over time is a spike train. These mechanisms are the basis of SNNs \cite{gerstner2014}. Because spikes are binary events, a spiking network can in principle replace multiplications by additions and does no transmission when there is no spike. This can be energy-efficient if activity is sparse and the hardware is designed to exploit it, as in neuromorphic processors.

\subsection{SNN fundamentals and the LIF model}

An SNN needs a mathematical model of the neuron. In this project it is the Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) model \cite{gerstner2014}. As its name indicates, it has three stages: integrate, leak and fire. First, the neuron accumulates the input from incoming spikes, which raises its membrane potential (integration). Second, the membrane is not a perfect integrator: if input spikes do not arrive often enough, the potential decays back toward its resting value (leak). Finally, when the potential reaches a threshold, the neuron emits a binary spike and its potential is reset to a baseline value (fire). In the proposed architecture, each continuous node generated by NEAT is replaced by an LIF neuron.

The LIF model can be written as an electrical circuit with membrane capacitance $C$, membrane resistance $R$, input current $I(t)$ and membrane potential $u(t)$ \cite{gerstner2014}:

\begin{equation}
    I(t) = I_R(t) + I_C(t), \qquad I_R(t) = \frac{u(t)-u_{\mathrm{rest}}}{R}, \qquad I_C(t) = C\frac{du(t)}{dt}.
\end{equation}

Combining these terms gives the membrane equation

\begin{equation}
    \tau_m\frac{du(t)}{dt} = -\left(u(t)-u_{\mathrm{rest}}\right) + R I(t), \qquad \tau_m = RC.
\end{equation}

When $u(t)$ reaches the threshold $\vartheta$, the neuron emits a spike and its potential is reset to $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$.

Simulations on a digital computer use a discrete-time version of this model, in which the potential is updated once per time step. In the form used in this project, the potential first accumulates the incoming current $I_t$, is then multiplied by a leak factor $\beta\in(0,1)$ (the discrete counterpart of the exponential decay with time constant $\tau_m$), and the neuron spikes if the result reaches the threshold:

\begin{equation}
    u_t = \beta\,\max\bigl(u_{t-1} + I_t,\; u_{\mathrm{rest}}\bigr), \qquad s_t = \begin{cases} 1, & u_t \ge \vartheta,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}

with $u_t$ reset to $u_{\mathrm{reset}}$ after a spike. The $\max$ keeps the potential from falling below the resting value, which is taken as zero (and equal to the reset value) in the implementation.

Combining NEAT with LIF neurons gives the proposed Spiking-NEAT architecture. This has a consequence for training: spikes are binary events and therefore not differentiable, so such networks cannot be trained directly with standard backpropagation, and gradient-based approaches need workarounds such as surrogate gradients. A Spiking-NEAT architecture therefore requires a learning rule that does not depend on gradients. Reward-modulated plasticity, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:rstdp}, is one such rule.

\subsection{Training spiking networks}

SNNs are difficult to train with standard backpropagation because of the non-differentiable nature of spikes. Surrogate-gradient methods \cite{neftci2019surrogate} address this by replacing the derivative of the spike function with a smooth approximation during the backward pass, which makes gradient-based optimisation possible; however, backpropagation through time requires storing the network's state over many time steps, which makes it costly for continuous, online learning. For an autonomous agent that must adapt while it acts, local learning rules, such as Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP) and its reward-modulated variant, are an attractive alternative, because each synapse is updated using only information available locally.

Integrating SNNs with neuroevolution is another way to obtain effective networks without gradients. For instance, Qiu et al.\ \cite{qiu2018evolving} applied NEAT to evolve recurrent spiking controllers for nonlinear control problems in continuous domains. In their work, evolution determined the weights and topology of the spiking network, which then stayed fixed during operation. Combining evolved topologies with local plasticity for adaptation within an episode has been explored only on small tasks (Section~\ref{sec:sota-epann}).

\subsection{Theoretical energy estimation}
\label{sec:energy-estimation}

The energy comparison in this project is limited to arithmetic operations at inference time. A conventional controller is modelled by its Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) operations and its activation-function evaluations, while the spiking controller is modelled by its Synaptic Operations (SynOps), its membrane updates and the input encoding:

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{ANN}} = N_{\mathrm{MAC}} E_{\mathrm{MAC}} + N_{\mathrm{act}} E_{\mathrm{act}},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{SNN}} = N_{\mathrm{SynOp}} E_{\mathrm{AC}} + N_{\mathrm{update}} E_{\mathrm{update}} + E_{\mathrm{encoding}}.
\end{equation}

Here $N_{\mathrm{MAC}}$ is the number of MAC operations, $N_{\mathrm{act}}$ the number of activation-function evaluations, $N_{\mathrm{SynOp}}$ the number of synaptic operations (one accumulate per spike delivered through a synapse), and $N_{\mathrm{update}}$ the number of membrane updates, each with its corresponding energy cost per operation.

Using the commonly cited 45-nm estimates of Horowitz \cite{horowitz2014computing}, a 32-bit floating-point MAC costs approximately $4.6\,\mathrm{pJ}$ and an accumulate (addition) costs approximately $0.9\,\mathrm{pJ}$. The reported efficiency metric is the ratio $E_{\mathrm{SNN}}/E_{\mathrm{ANN}}$, together with the operation counts used to obtain it.

In the SNN, $N_{\mathrm{update}}$ is the total number of membrane updates, in which the potential is multiplied by the leak factor at every time step. Because this requires a multiplication, $E_{\mathrm{update}}$ is conservatively treated as equal to a MAC ($4.6\,\mathrm{pJ}$), which avoids underestimating the SNN cost. Because the SNN processes spikes instead of raw pixel values, the operations needed to convert the observation grid into spike trains over the internal time steps are counted separately as $E_{\mathrm{encoding}}$, summed over the evaluation episode.

The model covers arithmetic only and ignores memory-access costs, even though moving data between memory and the processor typically consumes more energy than arithmetic. The ratio $E_{\mathrm{SNN}}/E_{\mathrm{ANN}}$ should therefore be read as a comparative estimate under explicit assumptions, not as a prediction of power consumption on a specific device.

\section{Synaptic Plasticity and Reward-Modulated Learning}

\subsection{Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP)}

The conventional architectures discussed so far rely on continuous activation functions and are trained by backpropagation, which computes gradients of a loss function and therefore requires continuous values. The Spiking-NEAT approach uses binary spikes, so gradient descent cannot be applied directly. Relying only on NEAT's random weight mutations between generations is a slow way to tune weights. This project therefore proposes a biologically inspired alternative that adapts the synaptic weights while the agent interacts with the environment.

In biological brains, learning is associated with synaptic plasticity, the ability of connections between neurons to strengthen or weaken depending on their activity. This idea goes back to the Hebbian postulate: if neuron A repeatedly and persistently takes part in firing neuron B, the efficacy of the synapse from A to B increases \cite{hebb1949,gerstner2014}. It is often summarised as ``neurons that fire together wire together''. Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP) is a formalisation in which the weight change depends on the precise timing of spikes. If a presynaptic neuron fires shortly before a postsynaptic neuron, the connection is strengthened (Long-Term Potentiation, LTP); if it fires shortly after, the connection is weakened (Long-Term Depression, LTD).

For a spike-time difference $s=t_{\mathrm{post}}-t_{\mathrm{pre}}$, a standard pair-based STDP learning window is

\begin{equation}
W(s) =
\begin{cases}
    A_{+}\exp(-s/\tau_{+}), & s>0,\\
    -A_{-}\exp(s/\tau_{-}), & s<0,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $A_{+}$ and $A_{-}$ control the amount of potentiation and depression, while $\tau_{+}$ and $\tau_{-}$ determine their temporal ranges. In R-STDP (next section), this window defines the pre-post contribution $F_{ij}(t)$ that feeds the eligibility trace.

Standard STDP is unsupervised: it considers only the relative timing of spikes and has no information about whether the resulting behaviour was good or bad for the agent. Consequently, if a harmful action is repeated, STDP can keep strengthening the pathways that produce it simply because the neurons fire in sequence. Without a goal-related signal, STDP alone can therefore reinforce unhelpful behaviour.

\subsection{Reward-Modulated STDP (R-STDP)}
\label{sec:rstdp}

Reward-modulated STDP (R-STDP) addresses this limitation by adding a reward signal \cite{mozafari2018,izhikevich2007,florian2007,legenstein2008,fremaux2016}. It addresses the \emph{distal reward problem}: a reward usually arrives some time after the spikes that caused it, so the network must remember which synapses were recently active. R-STDP expands standard STDP into a three-factor learning rule, whose factors are the presynaptic activity, the postsynaptic activity and a global neuromodulatory signal that plays a role similar to dopamine in biological brains. When a presynaptic and a postsynaptic spike occur in sequence, STDP does not change the weight immediately: it updates a temporary \emph{eligibility trace} on that synapse. The weight changes only when the neuromodulatory signal arrives, in proportion to the trace. If the agent receives more reward than expected, the traced synapses are strengthened; if it receives less than expected, they are weakened \cite{mozafari2018}. In this way, the learning is guided by the outcome of the agent's behaviour and not only by spike timing.

The eligibility trace and the reward-modulated weight update can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
    \frac{de_{ij}(t)}{dt} = -\frac{e_{ij}(t)}{\tau_e} + F_{ij}(t),
    \qquad
    \frac{dw_{ij}(t)}{dt} = d(t)e_{ij}(t),
\end{equation}

where $F_{ij}(t)$ is the instantaneous pre-post STDP contribution of the synapse from neuron $j$ to neuron $i$, $\tau_e$ is the trace time constant, and

\begin{equation}
    d(t) = R(t)-\langle R\rangle
\end{equation}

is the neuromodulatory reward-prediction error, that is, the difference between the current reward $R(t)$ and its running average $\langle R\rangle$. Positive values strengthen tagged synapses, and negative values weaken them.

In summary, the proposed model combines three components: NEAT acts as the architect that builds the network topology starting from minimal structures; LIF neurons are the processing engine, which uses binary spikes and can reduce the number of costly operations; and R-STDP is the teacher, a reward-driven local rule that adapts the synaptic weights during play according to behavioural success and not only spike timing.
